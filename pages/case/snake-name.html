SnakeName — AI snake identification app

SnakeName uses AI to recognise a snake's species from a single photo. As UI/UX designer on the project — my master's thesis — I owned market analysis, user research, feature definition and information architecture, UI design, an interactive prototype, and collaboration with the development team.

Problem

We framed the core problem simply: it's difficult to identify a snake quickly and reliably. Many species look nearly identical to a non-expert, and a wrong identification isn't just a labelling mistake — in some situations it can influence whether someone approaches the snake, calls for professional help, or seeks medical care.

Existing options were slow, regionally limited, or required expert knowledge — searching a field guide, waiting for a reply in an online group, or relying on an app with inconsistent accuracy. The design had to answer three questions: how to make identification as fast as possible, how to present an uncertain result without creating a false sense of certainty, and which information the user needs to see immediately.

Market & user research

Market analysis

I analysed AI-based identification apps, digital encyclopedias, Facebook groups, local rescue organisations and traditional field guides — comparing identification method, accuracy, geographic limits, response speed and UI quality. Automated apps were fast but sometimes inaccurate or dated; expert manual ID was more accurate but slow and often regional; groups depended on member availability; and field guides were thorough but too technical for a first-time user. Together this confirmed room for a tool combining automatic speed, global reach and well-presented information.

User research

I first defined eleven use cases — among them a resident of a snake-prone area, a tourist in unfamiliar terrain, a nature photographer, a herpetology enthusiast, a rescuer or guide, and a doctor working from a photo of a possible bite. I then ran an English-language online survey distributed through relevant communities, social media and paid promotion, gathering 137 responses from people in different countries.

Key finding 1

78.6% of respondents said they would use a reliable snake-identification app.

Key finding 2

Users wanted the most precise species match possible — or, failing that, a narrowed-down set of likely candidates rather than a single guess.

Key finding 3

71.4% wanted to know immediately whether the snake is venomous, alongside bite symptoms, venom type, extra photos, the scientific name and its range.

Key finding 4

Some respondents flagged offline functionality as important, since identification often happens without a signal, out in the field.

Design & prototype

Research findings became a prioritised feature list, then moved through paper sketches, low-fidelity wireframes, high-fidelity UI and a final interactive prototype.

Flow diagram

Wireframes & flow

First sketches on paper, focused purely on layout, navigation and content volume — not visual detail. This grew into 13 core wireframes across 4 sections (login, home & profile, identification flow, results & snake info), with a bottom navigation bar for home, starting an identification, and the user profile.

Wireframe

Visual identity & UI

Montserrat for its readability at small sizes; a primary green (#57DCA0) that links to nature but stays calmer than an alarming warning colour, keeping the app from unintentionally amplifying anxiety around a "dangerous animal" theme — red is reserved strictly for warnings. A reusable component set (buttons, navigation, cards, result items, form fields, icons, status indicators) kept every screen consistent and made handoff to developers straightforward.

Final UI

High fidelity UI example
High fidelity UI example

The 13 base wireframes grew into 31 high-fidelity screens, connected into an interactive Figma prototype covering the full journey from login to viewing a snake's detail. Notable iterations included increasing card and navigation contrast, simplifying the profile, moving rarely-used features into the account section, reworking the results hierarchy, adding an empty state before the first identification, and unifying icons and status colours.

Design decision: Never present a single "definitive" species when the model is not confident — a ranked "Similar species" screen with match percentages keeps the result honest and keeps the user in control.

Development & testing

After internal validation, the design was handed to the development team as the basis for implementation. During the build, I consulted on feature behaviour, checked fidelity against the design, and adjusted some components and colours based on feedback from the real environment — the outcome was not just a static concept, but a functional mobile app ready for final validation.

To validate usability, we prepared qualitative testing of the built app and invited 80 volunteers with experience of similar apps or an interest in snakes, covering overall impression, clarity of the identification process, quality of the results screen, completeness of the snake info, and any usability issues. The number of completed responses ended up too low for statistically reliable conclusions, so the results weren't presented as a full validation.

I treat this limitation as an honest and important part of the project — the design went through internal testing and implementation review, but a proper evaluation of wider public behaviour would need another round of moderated testing after public release.

Finished mobile application SnakeName

Outcome

The result is a complete design for a snake-identification app that connects AI recognition with clearly structured expert information. The most important contribution is how it handles an uncertain result: rather than presenting a single species as an unquestionable answer, it transparently offers several similar species and their match rate, so the user gets a fast response while understanding the limits of automatic recognition.
